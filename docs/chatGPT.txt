## 1) Where your (V_m,;V_w,;M_m,;M_w) enter and why they matter

Your sketch uses the simple momentum-transfer / scaling chain:

  [
  M_m v_0 \sim M_w v_j \quad\Rightarrow\quad v_j \sim \frac{M_m}{M_w} v_0
  ]
* then ballistic height:
  [

## 2) What (V_w) *should* represent physically

You already listed three contributors to the moving water: (i) the fluid participating in the **initial cavity collapse** that focus to the axis, (ii) contributions from **side-wall reflections**, and (iii) bottom/other reflections. Practically:

* **(V_w)** must be the *effective* volume (or mass) of liquid that ends up **participating in the focused axial jet** (i.e. the fluid that must be accelerated axially to create the jet).
* That effective volume is set by the **minimum radius of the collapsing neck** (call it (r_{\min})) and the characteristic axial length over which fluid is redirected into the jet. For dimensional scaling one convenient choice is
  [
  M_w \sim \rho_w, r_{\min}^3,
  ]
  i.e. the mass of a small control volume of radius (r_{\min}) and O((r_{\min})) axial size. This is exactly the kind of ansatz used to reduce the full flow field to a single effective mass in many jetting analyses (and is consistent with the reduced-model approach in Gekle & Gordillo). 

So the problem reduces to: **how does (r_{\min}) depend on (\alpha=r/R)?**

## 3) What Gekle & Gordillo say that matters for your modelling

Two points from their analysis are important:

1. the local axial velocity at jet base (and thus jet tip) scales like a *sink-strength* divided by a small lengthscale at the base: schematic relation (their model) is
   [
   u_0 \simeq B(\mathrm{Fr}),\frac{q_b}{r_b},
   ]
   where (q_b) (or (q_c)) is a measure of the collapsing flow/sink strength coming from the outer cavity and (r_b) (or (r_{\min}) in pinch-off literature) is the small radius at the base. This gives the mechanism for `focusing amplification`: small (r_b) → large (u_0). 

2. the *actual* small length (r_{\min}) is determined by the collapse dynamics (inertia vs hydrostatic / inertial self-similar collapse), and it **does not vanish** for a disk impact — there is a finite (r_{\min}) set by the flow rather than by the impactor inner radius. That is why disks produce finite jets despite strong focusing. 

(See especially the model relations in section 4 and the discussion of jet-base dynamics and sink-strength in their paper.)

## 4) A physically consistent ansatz for (r_{\min}(\alpha)) (and hence (M_w(\alpha)))

You observed the two limits:

* (\alpha\to1) (thin ring → (V_m\to0)) → (h_m\to0). So the geometry function must vanish there.
* (\alpha\to0) (solid disk) → (h_m) finite. So geometry function must be finite there.

The simplest way to enforce these constraints is:

**(A)** model the available energy (numerator) by ((R^2-r^2)) (or ((1-\alpha^2)) after normalization), and
**(B)** model the *effective* small length (r_{\min}(\alpha)) as a positive function that never goes to zero for (\alpha\to0) but can get smaller as (\alpha) increases (inner wall helps focusing).

A stable and flexible functional form that meets these constraints is

[
\boxed{ ; r_{\min}(\alpha) ;=; R;\big[\gamma_0 ;+; \gamma_1,(1-\alpha)^{p}\big]; , \qquad \gamma_0>0,; \gamma_1\ge0,; p>0; }
]

**Rationale**

* (\alpha=0) (disk): (r_{\min}=R(\gamma_0+\gamma_1)) — finite.
* (\alpha\to1) (thin ring): (r_{\min}\to R\gamma_0). If the inner-wall focusing effect (\gamma_1(1-\alpha)^p) vanishes, (r_{\min}) tends to a baseline (R\gamma_0) (small but finite). The jet strength still goes to zero in your full formula because the *available energy* factor ((R^2-r^2)^2) goes to zero faster (see below).
* (\gamma_1) controls how strongly the inner-wall (the ring) reduces (r_{\min}) compared to the disk baseline; (p) controls how rapidly that focusing turns on with ring width.

Now plug into your algebra. If you had (as in your sketch) something like

[
h_m \propto \Big(\frac{\rho_m}{\rho_w}\Big)^2 \frac{(R^2-r^2)^2}{r_{\min}^6}; H
]

(replace the particular power 6 with whatever your algebra gives — I reconstruct this from your steps where (M_w\sim\rho_w r_{\min}^3)), then substituting the ansatz gives a *finite* geometry function

[
\boxed{ ; G(\alpha)=
\frac{(1-\alpha^2)^2}{\big(\gamma_0+\gamma_1 (1-\alpha)^p\big)^6}; }
]

Properties:

* (\alpha\to0): (G(0) = 1/(\gamma_0+\gamma_1)^6) — finite.
* (\alpha\to1): numerator ((1-\alpha^2)^2\to0) → (G\to0) (so (h_m\to0)).
* For intermediate (\alpha), (G) can be large if the denominator is small (strong focusing) but it will not diverge to infinity as (\alpha\to0).

So this fixes the (\alpha^{-4}) blowup you found because you replaced the assumption (r_{\min}\propto r=\alpha R) (which gives (\alpha^{-4}) after algebra) by a physically motivated (r_{\min}(\alpha)) that does not collapse like (\alpha) for (\alpha\to0).
